Chunks

An English-learning app that starts from the sentence you just couldn't say.

Role
Design · Build · Daily user
Platform
iOS app · web PWA
Year
2026
Status
In daily use

The Problem

I could read anything. I still froze mid-sentence.

After two years in Sydney my English was fine on paper. In conversation I kept hitting the same wall: I knew exactly what I wanted to say in Chinese, and the natural English for it just wasn't there. I tried the obvious tools. Each one solved the wrong half of the problem.

The moment you fail to say something is the single best moment to learn it — you have the context, the motivation, and the exact sentence. Every existing tool threw that moment away. Chunks is built to catch it: ask once, and the phrases worth keeping become cards on their own.

  1. Vocabulary apps

    Someone else's list, in no context I'd ever meet. I learned words I never used and still lacked the ones I needed yesterday.

  2. Translator apps

    Perfect answer, zero retention. I'd paste it, send it, and need the same phrase again a week later.

  3. Flashcard apps

    The memory science works. The card-making doesn't — it's a chore, so the deck stays empty.

Design Details

Everything I took out, and the little I put in.

The design brief I gave myself: the app must cost less effort than not using it. Every detail below exists to remove a step, a decision, or a doubt.

  1. One box, one button

    No language picker, no mode switch, no settings on the main screen. The app knows you're typing Chinese and wants English back. Because the moment I'm trying to catch lasts about five seconds. Any choice on the way in and I'd just paste it into a translator instead.

  2. Saving is not a step

    There is no save button anywhere. Cards are written the instant the phrases come back; the green bar just tells you how many. Because 'decide whether to keep this' is exactly the chore that kills flashcard apps. Keeping everything and pruning later is cheaper than judging up front.

  3. Chunks, not sentences, not words

    A card is a phrase-sized piece of language with its meaning and one fresh example — never the whole sentence you typed, never a single word. Because whole sentences don't transfer to new situations and single words don't carry the grammar. Phrases are the unit fluent speakers actually reuse.

  4. Grading is a gesture

    Swipe right before flipping means it was easy. Flip, then swipe right, means you got it. Swipe left means you forgot. The edge of the screen glows green or red as you drag. Because buttons invite deliberation, and a swipe is over before you can second-guess it.

  5. Chinese on the front

    Cards show the Chinese first. Because the problem was production, not comprehension. I could already recognise ‘pick this up another day’. I needed to be able to reach for it.

  6. Search instead of categories

    The library has a search box and nothing else. There are no folders, tags, or scenes to maintain. Because I shipped auto-tagging, watched it be wrong, and noticed I only ever searched anyway. The story is in the iterations below.

  7. A fallback that never wastes the call

    If a sentence is too basic to yield phrases, the whole sentence becomes one card, and the app says so. Because 'nothing happened' is the worst possible response to someone who just made the effort to ask.

  8. Works offline, syncs quietly

    Cards live on the device. Sync happens a second and a half after you stop doing things, and silently gives up if there's no network. Because review happens on the train and the tram, and a spinner is a reason to close the app.

Iterations

What daily use changed.

I'm the only user, which is a weakness and a superpower: every decision got tested against real frustration within a day. These are the ones that flipped.

  1. Saved the sentence and the phrases

    The first version created a card for the full sentence plus one per phrase. Within a week the review queue felt padded — I was seeing the same idea twice in a row. Cut the sentence card. Phrases only; the sentence is kept as the card's example instead.

  2. Auto scene tags and a 'graph' tab

    Each card got an AI-assigned scene — 点餐, 职场吐槽 — and a fifth tab to browse by scene. I tuned the prompt hard: two to four characters, verbs not descriptions, a banned list of vague words. In use, the labels bent toward the sentence's mood: a neutral phrase from an anxious sentence got tagged 担忧. And I never browsed by scene — when I wanted a card, I searched. Removed the tab, the field, and half the prompt in the iOS rewrite. Wrote a 'do not rebuild this' rule into the project notes.

  3. Three buttons, then a real scheduler

    The first PWA used a simplified three-grade SM-2 because I could explain it in one sentence. Once the shape of the product was settled, both clients moved to FSRS: a card comes back when the chance of recalling it drops below 90%, and old cards had their history converted rather than reset.

  4. A native app, because the PWA proved the product

    After months of daily use the shape was fixed: four tabs, gestures for grading, offline first. That earned a SwiftUI rewrite. The web version was then rebuilt to match it, with the same scheduler and the same sync protocol.

Under The Hood

Small enough to hold in one head.

Two clients, one thin backend. The iOS app is SwiftUI and SwiftData. The web version is a vanilla-JS PWA with no build step and the same four tabs. Both talk to a single Cloudflare Worker that proxies the AI, keeps the keys on the server, and syncs cards to D1. Sync is offline first and last-write-wins, with soft deletes so every device learns about a removal.
